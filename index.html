I.How to Use This Guide

This is a tool, not an assignment. Open it any time you need help.

The Rome website is your main place to learn. This guide sits beside the site to help when something is hard to read, hard to see, or hard to start.

You can use it to:

  • Read a shorter version of what's on the site
  • Find out what a hard word means
  • See what a map or picture shows, in words
  • Get a sentence starter when writing feels stuck
  • Take a quick break and come back ready

How to read it

Use the menu at the top to jump to any section. You don't have to read it in order. You don't have to read all of it. Use what helps.

If you use a screen reader or headphones: this guide is built so the reader will pick up every word, including the parts that describe pictures and maps from the website.

IV.Pictures & Maps

The website has maps, charts, and pictures. Some of them won't read out loud well. Here is what each one shows, in words.

The Map of Rome's Growth

What this is

An interactive map. It shows how Rome grew from one small city into a giant empire. You can change the year on the map to see Rome at different times.

Here's what the map shows, step by step in time:

Around 753 BCE — The beginning

Rome is just one small city. It sits next to the Tiber River, in the middle of the Italian peninsula (the boot-shaped piece of land that sticks out into the Mediterranean Sea). Rome controls only the land right around the city.

By 270 BCE — All of Italy

Rome now controls the whole Italian peninsula. It took hundreds of years of fighting and making deals with other Italian peoples.

By 146 BCE — Across the sea

After the Punic Wars against Carthage (a city in North Africa), Rome controls big new pieces of land: the islands of Sicily, Sardinia, and Corsica. It also takes most of Spain, parts of North Africa, and Greece.

By 44 BCE — Caesar's time

Rome controls almost the whole edge of the Mediterranean Sea. Julius Caesar has just added Gaul (modern France). Rome also controls parts of the Middle East.

By 117 CE — The largest size

Rome is now an empire and reaches its biggest size ever. It stretches from Britain in the north to North Africa in the south, and from Spain in the west all the way to parts of modern Iraq in the east. Rome controls the entire Mediterranean Sea — Romans called it "Our Sea."

What you should notice

  • Rome started small and grew slowly at first.
  • The biggest growth came after the Punic Wars and during the early empire.
  • The Mediterranean Sea is in the middle of the empire. Rome used the sea like a giant highway.

V.The Big Idea

Everything in this unit comes back to one question.

How do political institutions
impact the advancement of civilizations? The Compelling Question

What that question really means

"Political institutions" is a fancy way to say kinds of government — like a kingdom, a republic, or an empire.

"Advancement of civilizations" means how a society grows and changes — how it gets bigger, stronger, or weaker.

So the question is really:

Does the kind of government a place has
change how that place grows?

The unit's answer (so far)

Rome had three kinds of government: monarchy, republic, and empire. Each one fixed some old problems but also made new problems. None of them was perfect. Rome kept changing because each system stopped working over time.

That's the main idea. If you can explain that idea, you understand the unit.

VI.The Three Stories of Rome

Rome's whole history can be told as three short stories. Read one. Or all three.

Story One

From One King to Many Voices (the Republic)

"We don't want a king. We want laws."

Rome started as a small city ruled by kings. The last king, Tarquin, was cruel. In 509 BCE, the Romans pushed him out. They said: never again will one person have all the power.

So they built a new system called a republic. In a republic, citizens vote for leaders. Two leaders called consuls ran the government. They could only serve for one year. The Senate, a group of important men, gave them advice. There were also assemblies, where regular citizens could vote on laws.

But there was a problem. Two groups of people lived in Rome: the patricians (rich families) and the plebeians (everyone else). The patricians had almost all the power. The plebeians fought for fairness.

Over many years, the plebeians won big things:

  • The Twelve Tables: Rome's first written laws, posted in public, so the rich couldn't make them up as they went.
  • The Tribunes of the Plebs: special leaders who could say "no" (called a veto) to laws that hurt regular people.
  • Step by step, plebeians got more rights to hold office and vote.
The republic solved the problem of one cruel king. But it created a new problem — fights between rich and poor that never really went away.
Story Two

Too Big, Too Fast (the Republic Breaks)

"Winning made everything worse."

Rome kept growing. It fought three big wars against Carthage, called the Punic Wars (264–146 BCE). Rome won them all. After that, Rome controlled most of the lands around the Mediterranean Sea.

Winning sounds good. But it caused new problems:

  • Rich Romans got much richer. They bought up huge farms (called latifundia) using enslaved people captured in war.
  • Regular Roman farmers couldn't compete. They lost their land. Many moved to the city of Rome, poor and angry.
  • Rome was now ruling lands far away. The republic was built for one city. It wasn't built to run an empire.

Some leaders tried to fix things. The Gracchi brothers tried to give land back to poor Romans. Both brothers were killed by their enemies in the Senate.

Then strong generals took over. Sulla marched his army into Rome itself — Romans had never done that before. Julius Caesar did it next. He crossed the Rubicon river in 49 BCE and started a civil war (Romans fighting Romans). Caesar won. He took total control.

In 44 BCE, senators killed Caesar because they were afraid he was becoming a king. But killing him didn't bring back the republic. Another civil war started.

The republic worked for a small Rome but couldn't handle a huge Rome. Growing too big, too fast broke the system.
Story Three

One Man Again (the Empire)

"Peace, but at a price."

After Caesar's death, his adopted son Octavian won the next civil war. In 27 BCE, the Senate gave him a new name: Augustus. He became Rome's first emperor.

Augustus was clever. He kept the Senate around. He kept calling himself "First Citizen," not "King." But really, he had all the power. The republic was over.

For the next 200 years, Rome was mostly peaceful. Historians call this time the Pax Romana ("Roman Peace"). During the Pax Romana:

  • Trade grew. Rome built thousands of miles of roads.
  • Cities grew. Many had aqueducts (stone water channels), public baths, and theaters.
  • Most of the empire was safe to travel through.

But there was a price. Citizens had less and less say in the government. Everything depended on having a good emperor — and not all of them were good.

Over time, the empire ran into big problems: not enough money, attacks from outside groups (like the Germanic tribes), and weak emperors. In 476 CE, the western half of the empire fell. The eastern half kept going for another thousand years.

The empire fixed the chaos of the late republic. But it traded freedom for safety, and depended on whoever was in charge.

VII.Vocabulary

The words that show up most in this unit, said in plain language.

Kinds of Government

monarchy
MON-ar-kee
A government ruled by one person, usually a king or queen.
republic
ree-PUB-lik
A government where people vote for leaders to represent them.
empire
EM-pire
A big group of lands all ruled by one government, usually with one strong leader called an emperor.
dictator
DIK-tay-tor
A leader with all the power. In early Rome, a dictator was chosen for a short time during emergencies.

Roman People & Groups

citizen
SIT-i-zen
An official member of Rome with rights, like the right to vote.
patrician
puh-TRISH-un
A member of one of Rome's rich, old, powerful families.
plebeian
pleh-BEE-un
A regular Roman — a farmer, worker, or merchant. Not from a rich family.
magistrate
MAJ-i-strate
A government official with a specific job, like a judge or money manager.

How the Republic Worked

Senate
SEN-it
A group of about 300 important Romans who advised leaders and helped run the government.
consul
KON-sul
One of two top leaders of the Roman Republic. They served for one year only.
tribune
TRIB-yoon
A leader chosen to protect plebeians. A tribune could say "no" (veto) to unfair laws.
assembly
uh-SEM-blee
A meeting where citizens voted on laws and leaders.
veto
VEE-toh
The power to say "no" to a law and stop it.
checks and balances
CHEKS and BAL-en-sez
A system where different parts of government keep each other from getting too powerful.

Laws & Reforms

Twelve Tables
TWELV TAY-bulz
Rome's first written laws. They were posted in public so everyone could see them.
reform
ree-FORM
A change made to fix a problem or make something more fair.

Wars, Trouble, & Big Names

Punic Wars
PYOO-nik WARZ
Three wars between Rome and Carthage (264–146 BCE). Rome won all three.
Carthage
KAR-thij
A powerful trading city in North Africa. Rome's biggest rival.
Hannibal
HAN-i-bul
A general from Carthage. He famously crossed the Alps mountains with elephants to attack Rome.
province
PRAH-vins
A piece of land Rome controlled but that wasn't part of Rome itself.
latifundia
lat-i-FUN-dee-uh
Huge farms owned by rich Romans, worked by enslaved people.
Gracchi brothers
GRAH-kee
Two brothers, Tiberius and Gaius, who tried to help poor Romans. Both were killed for it.
Julius Caesar
JOO-lee-us SEE-zer
A famous Roman general who took total power. Killed by senators in 44 BCE.
civil war
SIV-il WAR
A war fought between people of the same country.
Augustus
aw-GUS-tus
Caesar's adopted son. Rome's first emperor. (His original name was Octavian.)
Pax Romana
PAKS roh-MAH-nuh
"Roman Peace." About 200 years of mostly peace and stable empire.

VIII.Sentence Starters

When writing feels hard, pick a starter and finish it your own way.

For comparing the three governments

  • One thing that was the same about ___ and ___ is ___.
  • The biggest difference between ___ and ___ was ___.
  • In a monarchy, only ___ had power. In a republic, ___ also had power.
  • The republic ended because ___.
  • The empire was different from the republic because ___.

For talking about conflict

  • One conflict that changed Rome was ___.
  • Before this conflict, Rome was ___. After it, Rome was ___.
  • A problem that the conflict caused was ___.
  • This conflict mattered because ___.

For the Inquiry Lab (looking through different subjects)

  • From a history view, the most important thing is ___.
  • From a civics view, the government did ___.
  • From an economics view, the problem was about ___.
  • From a geography view, the location mattered because ___.

For giving your opinion

  • I think ___ because ___.
  • One reason I believe this is ___.
  • Some people might say ___, but I think ___.
  • The best evidence for my idea is ___.
